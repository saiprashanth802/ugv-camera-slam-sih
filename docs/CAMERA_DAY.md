# Camera Day — field card for whoever holds the phone

**Audience: a non-engineer.** No knowledge of the project is needed. Follow the order
below and it works.

**Status.** Issued 2026-09-05 (also published as an Artifact — "Sightline Camera Day").
A shoot was done against it on 2026-09-06; **it did not follow this card**, and the
consequences are recorded in `RUNBOOK.md` §7e. This document stays as written, because it
is still the instruction for the reshoot that §7e argues for. If you are the person
shooting, read only this file — §7e is for the person processing the footage.

Two things to shoot with one phone: a short set of photos, then one steady outdoor walk.

---

## Before you leave — set the phone once, then don't touch it

Everything here depends on one rule: **the phone must behave identically for the photos
and for the walk.** Set it up at home, then leave the settings alone all day.

- [ ] Pick **one camera lens** — the normal one. Not ultra-wide, not the zoom lens.
- [ ] Set **zoom to 1×** and don't pinch-zoom at any point today.
- [ ] Pick **one video quality** setting and keep it for every take.
- [ ] Turn **off** any "auto framing", "cinematic", or subject-tracking mode.
- [ ] Clean the lens. Check the battery. Free up space — video is big.

Bring the phone and the gimbal, charged. No printer needed — the checkerboard goes on a
screen. Ask whoever sent you this for the file `calib_board_9x6.png` (it lives at
`media/calib_board_9x6.png`) and put it on the laptop before you start.

## Part one — photos of the checkerboard (~15 minutes, indoors)

These teach the software the exact shape of your phone's lens. Nobody will ever see them;
they are not part of the demo. **No printing required — the checkerboard goes on a screen.**

1. **Open the checkerboard image full-screen on the biggest screen you have.** A laptop or
   desktop monitor is ideal; a tablet works. A phone screen is too small.
   Full-screen means full-screen — no window edges, no toolbar, no wallpaper showing
   around it. Press F11, or double-click the image in a photo viewer.
2. **Turn the brightness up and stop the screen sleeping.** Disable screen saver, sleep and
   auto-dim for the next fifteen minutes. If the screen dims halfway through, those photos
   are wasted.
3. **Dim the room and check for reflections.** Stand where no lamp, window or ceiling light
   is bouncing off the glass. A reflection lying across the squares kills that photo.
4. **Take about 20 photos, each from a different position.** Move yourself, not the screen:
   closer, further back, from the left, from the right, from above, from below, tilted.
   The point is that no two photos look the same — twenty near-identical ones are worth
   less than eight varied ones.
5. **In every shot: the whole board inside the frame**, all four corners visible, filling a
   good chunk of the picture rather than sitting small in the middle. Tap to focus before
   each one.
6. **Keep the tilt moderate — up to about 45°.** Past that the screen washes out at an angle
   and the pattern stops being readable, which a printed sheet wouldn't do.
7. **Watch for shimmer.** If wavy rainbow patterns appear over the squares, shift slightly
   closer or further away and retake that shot.
8. **Don't shut the laptop yet.** These photos get checked and occasionally fail, in which
   case you simply shoot them again. Leave the setup as it is until someone confirms they
   passed.

**Why a screen beats a printout.** The usual way this step fails is a printed sheet that
curls a millimetre or two, which quietly corrupts the result without looking wrong. A
monitor is perfectly flat and perfectly rigid, so that failure can't happen. The only thing
it adds is glare, and glare is visible — you can see it and move.

**No second screen at all?** A real chess or draughts board works too — flat, rigid, high
contrast. Say so when you send the photos, because it has a different number of squares and
the settings have to be changed to match.

## Part two — the outdoor walk

One continuous video of you walking a loop outdoors. Same phone, same settings as the photos.

1. **Go out in daylight.** Broad daylight, outdoors. Not dusk, not indoors, not under
   floodlights.
2. **Put the phone on the gimbal and switch the gimbal on.** This matters more than anything
   else on the card. Handheld footage usually fails.
3. **Pick a loop you can walk back to the start of** — around a building, a courtyard, a
   block. Note where you started so you can return to the same spot.
4. **Hit record and walk it at a slow, even pace**, camera pointed forward and roughly level.
   Finish standing where you began, then stop recording. Aim for two to four minutes. Shoot
   the loop two or three times so there's a spare take.

| Do | Don't |
|---|---|
| Keep **walking forward while you turn** a corner — a wide, rounded turn. | **Never spin on the spot.** Turning while standing still is the single fastest way to ruin the clip. |
| Point the camera at things with **detail and texture**: brickwork, hedges, parked bikes, kerbs, path edges, signage. | No blank walls, plain sky, still water, or wide empty tarmac filling the frame. |
| Keep the horizon roughly level and the framing steady. | Avoid busy traffic or crowds taking up most of the picture. |
| Keep **one unbroken take** from start back to start. | No zooming, no switching lenses, no pausing and resuming mid-take. |

**Why the spinning rule exists.** The software has one camera and no GPS. It works out where
you were purely by watching how the scene slides past the lens as you move. Walking forward
gives it that movement. Turning on the spot gives it a scene that swings by with no actual
travel behind it — so it loses track of where you are, and the take is dead from that point on.

## What to send back

- The **~20 checkerboard photos**, as original files — not screenshots, not resized, not sent
  through a chat app that compresses them.
- The **walk videos**, all takes, also as original files.
- A one-line note: which phone, **what you showed the checkerboard on** (laptop, tablet, real
  chess board), and which take you think is the best.

Send them by AirDrop, USB cable, or a cloud link — anything that transfers the file untouched.
**Messaging apps shrink both photos and video and make them unusable here.**
